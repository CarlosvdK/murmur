# Decision Making -- Key Insights

## Insight from: simon1955bounded
**Finding:** People satisfice rather than optimise. They evaluate options against an aspiration level and pick the first one that meets it, rather than searching exhaustively for the best. Aspiration levels adjust up when options exceed them and down after repeated disappointment.
**Strength:** Very High (Simon, Nobel-prize work; foundational for bounded rationality)
**Application:** Personas should not exhaustively compare all options. They should pick the first "good enough" choice, especially under time pressure or low stakes. Rich-choice sets do not improve outcomes -- they slow decisions and can trigger avoidance.
**Prompt fragment:** "Personas satisfice -- they pick the first option that passes a personal bar, not the absolute best. Rich option sets slow decisions and can trigger avoidance rather than deeper comparison."

## Insight from: iyengar2000choice
**Finding:** More choices can reduce purchase rates and satisfaction. The classic jam study: 24-flavour displays drew more foot traffic but 30% fewer purchases than a 6-flavour display. Across later replications, choice overload effects appear at approximately 5-9 options depending on category familiarity.
**Strength:** High (widely cited, mixed replication but the direction is consistent in low-familiarity domains)
**Application:** Removing options from menus, subscription tiers, or flows can increase conversion in low-familiarity categories. Resist the persona instinct that "more options = better service" -- for purchase decisions, fewer often converts better.
**Prompt fragment:** "Choice overload: above ~5-9 options in unfamiliar categories, adding more options reduces conversion and satisfaction. Personas will say they want more options but convert at a lower rate."

## Insight from: kahneman2011thinking (System 1 / System 2)
**Finding:** Decisions are made by two systems: fast, automatic, pattern-driven System 1 and slow, deliberative, effortful System 2. Most everyday consumer decisions are made by System 1; System 2 only engages when the situation is novel, effortful, or flagged as risky.
**Strength:** Very High (Kahneman, Nobel-prize work)
**Application:** Low-stakes, habitual, or distracted consumer decisions (most of Murmur's territory) are System 1. Persona predictions that rely on careful trade-off thinking overstate how much thought real customers will give.
**Prompt fragment:** "Most consumer decisions are System 1: fast, habitual, pattern-driven. Personas answering thoughtfully about a System 1 situation will overstate the amount of deliberation real customers apply."

## Insight from: schwartz2004paradox / botti2015counterproductive
**Finding:** Maximisers (who search for the best option) report lower decision satisfaction than satisficers, even when they objectively choose better. More time deliberating increases post-decision regret.
**Strength:** High (Schwartz; multiple replications and refinements)
**Application:** Personas with maximiser tendencies (conscientious, deliberate decision style) will express more ambivalence about changes and more post-change regret even when the change benefits them. Build in persona diversity across maximiser/satisficer.
**Prompt fragment:** "Some personas are maximisers -- longer deliberation, higher regret. Others are satisficers -- faster decisions, higher satisfaction. Mix both. Maximisers will over-articulate downsides even for good changes."

## Insight from: johnson2012beyond
**Finding:** Choice architecture -- the way options are presented, ordered, grouped, and defaulted -- drives decisions as strongly as the option content itself. The same options under different architectures produce different choices by 20-50%.
**Strength:** Very High (foundation of nudge research; hundreds of field studies)
**Application:** When simulating a change to ordering, grouping, defaults, or visual emphasis, the persona reaction depends on architectural details the business owner may not have mentioned.
**Prompt fragment:** "Choice architecture matters as much as content. Ordering, grouping, defaults, and visual emphasis drive 20-50% swings in choice. If the business is changing architecture (not just content), weight those effects heavily."

## Insight from: bettman1998constructive
**Finding:** People do not have stable preferences they retrieve -- they CONSTRUCT preferences at the moment of choice based on available cues. The same person can prefer A over B in one context and B over A in another, depending on how options are presented and what comparisons are visible.
**Strength:** Very High (*Journal of Consumer Research*, foundational)
**Application:** Persona preferences are not fixed database lookups. They are constructed from context. Survey-style questions that force a context-free ranking are less predictive than scenario-embedded questions.
**Prompt fragment:** "Preferences are constructed, not retrieved. A persona's stated preference depends on what was presented alongside the option. Context-free rankings are less predictive than embedded-scenario responses."

## Insight from: thaler1980positive / camerer1999effect
**Finding:** Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equivalent gains (loss aversion ratio ~2:1). This is why status-quo bias and endowment effects are so strong: any change that could make things worse is weighted 2x a change that could make things equally better.
**Strength:** Very High (Kahneman & Tversky; replicated across many domains)
**Application:** When simulating a change that carries both upside and downside risk, persona reactions weight the downside ~2x. Fear-of-loss framing consistently beats equivalent gain framing.
**Prompt fragment:** "Loss aversion ~2:1. Any proposed change that carries downside risk is weighed roughly twice as heavily as equivalent upside."

## Insight from: gilbert2002durability / wilson2003affective
**Finding:** People systematically over-predict the intensity and duration of their own future emotional reactions -- positive and negative. Impact bias: we think a change will affect us more and for longer than it actually does.
**Strength:** High (Gilbert et al., widely replicated)
**Application:** Personas asked "how would you feel about this change?" will overstate the magnitude and duration of their reaction. By the third interaction with a change, most personas have adapted. The depth turn in Murmur's focus group explicitly exploits this.
**Prompt fragment:** "Impact bias: personas overstate both the intensity and duration of their reaction to a change. Most reactions attenuate sharply by the third interaction. Design prompts to surface adaptation, not just initial reaction."
