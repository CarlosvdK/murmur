# Consumer Psychology -- Key Insights

## Insight from: kahneman1979prospect
**Finding:** Losses are weighted ~2x more than equivalent gains. A 10% price increase causes roughly twice the emotional response of a 10% price decrease. This asymmetry is universal across cultures but varies in magnitude.
**Strength:** Very High (85,000 citations, Nobel Prize winning)
**Application:** All price increase simulations must weight negative reactions ~2x stronger than positive reactions to equivalent decreases.
**Prompt fragment:** "When this persona considers a price increase, their negative reaction should be approximately twice as strong as their positive reaction would be to an equivalent decrease. This is loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979)."

## Insight from: thaler1985mental
**Finding:** Consumers categorize spending into mental accounts. A 5 EUR increase on a 15 EUR lunch feels more painful than a 5 EUR increase on a 150 EUR dinner, even though the absolute amount is identical. Relative framing dominates.
**Strength:** Very High (9,500 citations)
**Application:** Price sensitivity in simulations must be relative to the baseline price, not absolute. Small businesses with low price points will see disproportionate reaction to small increases.
**Prompt fragment:** "This persona evaluates price changes relative to their mental account for this type of spending, not in absolute terms. A 1 EUR increase on a 4 EUR coffee (25%) feels larger than a 5 EUR increase on a 50 EUR dinner (10%)."

## Insight from: cialdini1984influence
**Finding:** Six principles drive consumer behavior: reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. For small businesses, social proof and liking/familiarity are strongest.
**Strength:** Very High (20,000+ citations)
**Application:** Personas who feel personally connected to the business (liking principle) are 3-4x more resistant to switching. Social proof amplifies both positive and negative reactions.
**Prompt fragment:** "If this persona has a personal relationship with the business owner or staff, apply the liking principle -- they are 3-4x less likely to switch and more likely to tolerate negative changes."

## Insight from: oliver1999loyalty
**Finding:** True loyalty requires both behavioral (repeat purchase) and attitudinal (emotional attachment) components. Most "loyal" customers are actually habitual -- they repeat-purchase without emotional attachment and will switch when friction is low enough.
**Strength:** High (5,000+ citations, Journal of Marketing)
**Application:** Distinguish between habitual customers (visit frequency driven) and truly loyal customers (emotional attachment driven). Habitual customers are more vulnerable to disruption.
**Prompt fragment:** "Determine whether this persona is habitually loyal (comes because convenient/routine) or attitudinally loyal (comes because emotionally attached). Habitual loyalty breaks more easily under disruption."

## Insight from: zeithaml1988perceptions
**Finding:** Perceived value = perceived quality / perceived price. Consumers don't evaluate price in isolation -- they evaluate what they get for what they pay. A price increase with visible quality improvement is 60% more acceptable than one without.
**Strength:** Very High (15,000+ citations)
**Application:** If a business change includes visible improvements alongside price increases, acceptance should be significantly higher. "Naked" price increases without justification trigger strongest resistance.
**Prompt fragment:** "If the price change comes with a visible quality or experience improvement, reduce resistance by ~40%. If it's a 'naked' price increase with no added value, increase resistance by ~30%."
