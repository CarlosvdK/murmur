# Convenience and Friction: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer reactions to ease-of-use, service speed, payment methods, and accessibility, apply the following empirically-grounded patterns. Friction is one of the strongest predictors of customer behavior -- small barriers produce outsized effects on conversion and satisfaction.

## Friction as Decision Barrier (Soman, "The Effect of Payment Transparency on Consumption," Journal of Marketing Research, 2003; Thaler & Sunstein, "Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness," 2008)

Even minor friction points dramatically reduce follow-through. Soman demonstrated that when payment mechanisms make costs more salient (transparent), consumption drops by 15-25%. Thaler and Sunstein documented how small changes in default options and process steps produce 20-40 percentage point swings in behavior. When simulating:
- Every additional step in a purchase or signup process should reduce completion by approximately 10-15% of remaining personas at each step. A 5-step checkout loses roughly 40-55% of initial intenders.
- Friction effects are asymmetric: removing a friction point produces a smaller gain than introducing one produces a loss. Eliminating one step may recover 5-8% of lost customers, but adding one step loses 10-15%.
- Personas with low motivation or casual interest are disproportionately affected. High-motivation personas (urgent need, strong brand loyalty) tolerate 2-3x more friction before abandoning.

## Service Convenience (Berry, Seiders, & Grewal, "Understanding Service Convenience," Journal of Marketing, 2002; Seiders, Voss, Godfrey, & Grewal, "SERVCON: Development and Validation of a Multidimensional Service Convenience Scale," Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 2007)

Berry et al. identified five dimensions of service convenience: decision, access, transaction, benefit, and post-benefit. Each dimension independently affects satisfaction and repurchase intent. Seiders et al. validated that service convenience explains 15-20% of variance in customer satisfaction beyond service quality alone. When simulating:
- Personas evaluate convenience across all five dimensions, not just speed. A fast service that is hard to find (access) or difficult to pay for (transaction) still scores poorly on overall convenience.
- Convenience expectations scale with price. Personas paying premium prices expect ~30-40% higher convenience than those paying budget prices.
- Time-poor personas (working parents, high-income professionals) weight convenience 1.5-2x more heavily than time-rich personas (retirees, students) in their overall evaluation.

## Self-Service vs Full-Service Tradeoffs (Meuter, Ostrom, Roundtree, & Bitner, "Self-Service Technologies: Understanding Customer Satisfaction with Technology-Based Service Encounters," Journal of Marketing, 2000; Meuter, Bitner, Ostrom, & Brown, "Choosing Among Alternative Service Delivery Modes," Journal of Marketing, 2005)

Self-service technologies (kiosks, apps, online ordering) satisfy customers when they work smoothly but produce stronger dissatisfaction than human service failures when they break down. Meuter et al. (2000) found that technology failures generated more intense negative emotions than equivalent human service failures. Meuter et al. (2005) identified technology anxiety, need for interaction, and prior experience as key moderators. When simulating:
- Personas high in technology comfort (typically younger, higher education) should prefer self-service options and report 15-20% higher satisfaction when they function well.
- Personas high in need for human interaction (typically older, higher Agreeableness) should resist self-service and report dissatisfaction even when the technology works correctly.
- When self-service fails (app crashes, kiosk errors), affected personas should report 1.5-2x the dissatisfaction of an equivalent human service failure. The persona feels both frustrated by the failure and abandoned by the lack of a human fallback.

## Delivery Speed and Willingness to Pay (Fisher, Gallino, & Xu, "The Value of Rapid Delivery in Omnichannel Retailing," Journal of Marketing Research, 2019; Hubner, Kuhn, & Wollenburg, "Last Mile Fulfilment and Distribution in Omni-Channel Grocery Retailing," International Journal of Retail and Distribution Management, 2016)

Fisher et al. found that same-day delivery increases conversion rates by 3-5 percentage points compared to standard shipping, and that customers are willing to pay $5-8 premiums for next-day delivery on orders averaging $50-75. Hubner et al. documented that delivery speed expectations vary significantly by product category: groceries demand same-day, general merchandise tolerates 2-3 days, and specialty items tolerate 5-7 days. When simulating:
- Urgency of the purchase should modulate delivery speed sensitivity. Personas buying gifts near a deadline or replacing broken essentials should weight speed 3-4x higher than those making planned purchases.
- Willingness to pay for faster delivery follows a curve: roughly 40-50% of personas will pay $5-8 for next-day, but only 15-20% will pay $15+ for same-day.
- Free shipping thresholds drive basket-building behavior. When told "free shipping at $X," roughly 60-70% of personas with carts below the threshold will consider adding items rather than paying for shipping.

## Payment Friction and Spending (Prelec & Simester, "Always Leave Home Without It: A Further Investigation of the Credit-Card Effect on Willingness to Pay," Marketing Letters, 2001; Raghubir & Srivastava, "Monopoly Money: The Effect of Payment Mechanism on Consumption," Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 2008; Soman, "Effects of Payment Mechanism on Spending Behavior," Journal of Consumer Research, 2001)

Less tangible payment methods increase spending. Prelec and Simester found that credit card users bid 50-100% more than cash users for identical items in controlled auctions. Raghubir and Srivastava showed that less transparent payment forms (gift cards, store credit, points) increase spending by 20-35% compared to cash equivalents. Soman found that pre-paid and decoupled payments reduce the pain of paying, increasing consumption. When simulating:
- Personas paying with cash or direct debit should exhibit 15-25% more price sensitivity than those paying with credit cards, mobile payments, or stored credits.
- Subscription and pre-paid models should reduce per-use price sensitivity. A persona on a monthly subscription should evaluate individual purchases within that subscription ~30-40% less critically on price.
- Introducing a new, more transparent payment method (e.g., switching from bundled to itemized billing) should increase price complaints by 20-30% even when total cost is unchanged.

## Location Convenience vs Destination Appeal (Huff, "Defining and Estimating a Trading Area," Journal of Marketing, 1964; Reimers & Clulow, "Retail Concentration: A Comparison of Spatial Convenience in Shopping Strips and Shopping Centres," Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 2009)

Huff's gravity model shows that store patronage probability is proportional to store attractiveness and inversely proportional to the square of travel time. Reimers and Clulow found that location convenience remains the primary driver for routine purchases, while destination appeal dominates for specialty and experiential purchases. When simulating:
- For everyday purchases (groceries, coffee, gas), a persona should weight location convenience 2-3x more than product quality or price differences of less than 15%.
- For specialty purchases (restaurants, clothing, services), personas will tolerate 2-3x more travel time than for routine purchases, but only when the perceived quality difference justifies it.
- A 10-minute increase in travel time should reduce patronage probability by roughly 30-40% for convenience-oriented businesses and 10-15% for destination businesses.
