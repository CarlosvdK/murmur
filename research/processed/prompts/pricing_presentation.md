# Pricing Presentation: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer reactions to how prices are displayed, structured, and framed, apply the following empirically-grounded patterns. Price presentation often matters as much as the price itself -- identical costs produce different reactions depending on format, context, and framing.

## Menu Cost Display Formats (Yang, Kimes, & Sessarego, "Menu Price Presentation Influences on Consumer Purchase Behavior in Restaurants," International Journal of Hospitality Management, 2009; Wansink, Painter, & van Ittersum, "Descriptive Menu Labels' Effect on Sales," Cornell Hotel and Restaurant Administration Quarterly, 2012)

Yang et al. found that removing dollar signs and decimal points from menu prices (e.g., displaying "14" instead of "$14.00") increased average spending by approximately 8-12% per check. The dollar sign activates the "pain of paying" more than the number alone. Wansink et al. found that descriptive menu labels ("Grandma's homestyle chicken" vs. "chicken") increased sales of described items by 27% and increased post-meal quality ratings. When simulating:
- Personas exposed to prices without currency symbols should report approximately 8-12% lower price sensitivity than those seeing explicit currency formatting.
- This effect is strongest for experiential purchases (dining, entertainment) and weakest for utilitarian purchases where personas are actively comparison-shopping.
- Descriptive framing around a product should increase perceived value by 15-25%, independent of actual product quality. Personas should rate a described item higher even when the underlying offering is identical.

## Decoy Effect and Asymmetric Dominance (Huber, Payne, & Puto, "Adding Asymmetrically Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis," Journal of Consumer Research, 1982; Simonson, "Choice Based on Reasons: The Case of Attraction and Compromise Effects," Journal of Consumer Research, 1989)

Introducing a clearly inferior "decoy" option shifts preference toward the option the decoy makes look good. Huber et al. demonstrated that adding an asymmetrically dominated alternative increased choice of the dominating option by 20-40 percentage points. Simonson showed that consumers also gravitate toward "compromise" options (middle of the range), with the middle option gaining 15-25 percentage points when extreme options are added. When simulating:
- When three pricing tiers are presented, roughly 50-60% of personas should choose the middle option, versus 25-35% choosing the middle when only two options are available.
- A decoy option (clearly worse than one alternative but not the other) should shift 15-30% of personas toward the dominating option.
- Personas with high analytical ability (high Openness, high education) are approximately 20-30% less susceptible to the decoy effect than the average persona, but not immune.
- The decoy effect is stronger for unfamiliar products where personas lack established preferences to anchor on.

## Price Presentation Order (Suk, Lee, & Lichtenstein, "The Influence of Price Presentation Order on Consumer Choice," Journal of Marketing Research, 2012; Karmarkar, Shiv, & Knutson, "Cost Conscious? The Neural and Behavioral Impact of Price Primacy on Decision Making," Journal of Marketing Research, 2015)

Suk et al. found that presenting prices in descending order (high to low) increases average spending by 10-15% compared to ascending order, because the first price encountered sets the anchor. Karmarkar et al. used neuroimaging to show that seeing price before seeing the product activates cost-evaluation circuits, reducing perceived product attractiveness. When simulating:
- Personas who see the highest-priced option first should anchor on it, making subsequent lower options feel more reasonable. Average selection should shift up by 10-15%.
- When price is presented before the product description (price-first framing), personas should be approximately 15-20% more price-sensitive than when product is presented first (product-first framing).
- For businesses wanting to sell premium options, simulate descending price presentation producing higher average order values. For businesses emphasizing value, ascending order should reinforce the affordability message.

## Comparative Pricing and Reference Price Effects (Grewal, Monroe, & Krishnan, "The Effects of Price-Comparison Advertising on Buyers' Perceptions of Acquisition Value, Transaction Value, and Behavioral Intentions," Journal of Marketing, 1998; Biswas & Blair, "Contextual Effects of Reference Prices in Retail Advertisements," Journal of Marketing, 1991)

"Was/now" pricing creates perceived transaction value independent of actual savings. Grewal et al. found that comparative price advertising increased purchase intent by 20-35% when the reference price was perceived as credible. Biswas and Blair found that exaggerated reference prices ("was $200, now $50") increase skepticism and reduce effectiveness by 25-40% compared to plausible reference prices. When simulating:
- A "was $X, now $Y" frame should increase purchase intent by 15-25% when the discount is 15-30% and the reference price is plausible.
- When discounts exceed 50%, roughly 30-45% of personas should express skepticism about the original price or the product quality. The credibility threshold varies by product category: luxury goods tolerate smaller discounts before triggering skepticism than commodity goods.
- Personas who are frequent shoppers or experienced comparison shoppers should discount reference prices by 30-50% (they know "was" prices are often inflated).

## Pay-What-You-Want Pricing (Kim, Natter, & Spann, "Pay What You Want: A New Participative Pricing Mechanism," Journal of Marketing, 2009; Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson, & Brown, "Shared Social Responsibility: A Field Experiment in Pay-What-You-Want Pricing and Charitable Giving," Science, 2012)

Kim et al. found that PWYW generates lower per-unit revenue (customers pay approximately 50-85% of the posted price on average) but can increase volume and customer acquisition. Gneezy et al. showed that linking PWYW to charity increased both participation rates and average payments by 20-30%, but only when the charitable component felt genuine. When simulating:
- Under PWYW, personas should cluster around three payment levels: freeloaders (10-15% paying near zero), fair payers (60-70% paying 40-70% of perceived fair price), and generous payers (15-20% paying at or above full price).
- Social pressure increases payments: visible payment settings should shift average payments up by 15-25% compared to anonymous settings.
- Repeat PWYW customers should gradually decrease payments over time by approximately 5-10% per visit as the novelty and social pressure diminish.
- Personas with high guilt-proneness (high Agreeableness, high Conscientiousness) should pay 20-40% more than average under PWYW.

## Subscription vs One-Time Pricing Framing (Gourville, "Pennies-a-Day: The Effect of Temporal Reframing on Transaction Evaluation," Journal of Consumer Research, 1998)

Gourville demonstrated that reframing an aggregate cost as a small daily amount ("pennies a day") increases acceptance by 20-35%. A $365/year fee reframed as "$1/day" felt more affordable even though the cost is identical. The effect works by shifting the comparison frame from large purchases (rent, car payments) to small daily expenses (coffee, snacks). When simulating:
- When a subscription is framed as a daily or weekly cost rather than monthly or annual, acceptance should increase by 15-25%.
- The framing is most effective for costs in the $100-500/year range. Below $100/year, the cost is already perceived as small. Above $500/year, personas recognize the reframing tactic and discount it.
- Annual billing presented alongside monthly equivalent ("$120/year -- just $10/month") should outperform monthly billing presented alone, because personas perceive the annual commitment as a savings opportunity and the monthly reframe as affordable.
- Approximately 20-30% of analytical or skeptical personas should be resistant to temporal reframing, mentally converting back to the total cost regardless of how it is presented.
