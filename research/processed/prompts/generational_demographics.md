# Generational & Demographic Effects: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer personas, modulate behavior by generational cohort, gender, and family structure. These demographic factors produce systematic, documented differences in purchasing patterns, brand relationships, and decision-making processes. Do not treat all personas as interchangeable consumers differing only in income.

## Generational Cohort Theory (Strauss & Howe, "Generations: The History of America's Future, 1584 to 2069," William Morrow, 1991; Parment, "Generation Y vs. Baby Boomers: Shopping Behavior, Buyer Involvement and Implications for Retailing," Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 2013)

Generational cohorts share formative experiences that produce lasting consumption patterns. Parment found that Gen Y (Millennials) shows 40-50% less brand loyalty than Baby Boomers, seeks 3x more information sources before purchasing, and values experience over ownership at roughly 2x the rate of older cohorts. Baby Boomers show 30-40% higher brand loyalty and are 2x more likely to choose familiar brands over novel alternatives.

When simulating: assign each persona a generational cohort and modulate their brand loyalty, information-seeking behavior, and openness to new businesses accordingly. Younger cohorts should show more exploratory behavior and less default loyalty; older cohorts should show stronger incumbency advantage for established businesses.

## Gen Z Consumer Behavior (Priporas, Stylos, & Fotiadis, "Generation Z Consumers' Expectations of Interactions in Smart Retailing: A Future Agenda," Computers in Human Behavior, 2017; Francis & Hoefel, "True Gen: Generation Z and Its Implications for Companies," McKinsey & Company, 2018)

Gen Z (born ~1997-2012) values authenticity above polish -- 67% prefer brands that show real, unfiltered content over professionally produced marketing. They are 2-3x more likely than older cohorts to research a business on social media before visiting. Price sensitivity is high (limited disposable income), but they will pay 10-15% premiums for brands that align with their social values (sustainability, inclusivity, transparency). Mobile-first behavior is default: 75-80% of Gen Z discovery and evaluation happens on smartphones.

When simulating: Gen Z personas should evaluate businesses heavily through social media presence and peer reviews. They should be skeptical of traditional advertising, responsive to authentic storytelling, and willing to switch providers quickly if values misalign. Their purchasing is mobile-dominant and impulsive in digital channels but deliberate for higher-price items.

## Millennial Purchasing Patterns (Ordun, "Millennial (Gen Y) Consumer Behavior, Their Shopping Preferences and Perceptual Maps Associated with Brand Loyalty," Canadian Social Science, 2015; Smith, "An Examination of Millennial Values and Generational Differences," Gallup, 2012)

Millennials (born ~1981-1996) prioritize experiences over material goods at a 3:1 ratio compared to Baby Boomers. They are the most subscription-oriented generation -- 70-75% hold at least one paid subscription service. Brand loyalty exists but is conditional: 60% will switch brands after a single bad experience vs. 35% of Boomers. Millennials are 2x more likely to share positive business experiences on social media than older generations, making them high-value word-of-mouth amplifiers.

When simulating: Millennial personas should show strong preference for experience-based offerings, subscription models, and convenience features. They should be vocal (both positive and negative) on social media channels. Their loyalty should be modeled as earned and fragile rather than habitual.

## Age and Brand Loyalty Differences (Lambert-Pandraud & Laurent, "Why Do Older Consumers Buy Older Brands? The Role of Attachment and Declining Innovativeness," Journal of Marketing, 2010)

Older consumers (55+) are 2-3x more likely to repurchase their current brand than younger consumers. This is driven by both emotional attachment (which increases with years of use) and declining innovativeness (willingness to try new things decreases by approximately 5% per decade after age 30). Lambert-Pandraud & Laurent found that for automobiles, the probability of buying the same brand again was 35% for adults under 40 and 60% for adults over 65.

When simulating: older personas should show strong inertia toward current providers and resistance to switching, even when presented with objectively superior alternatives. The switching cost is psychological, not financial. Younger personas should show lower default loyalty but can develop strong attachment through repeated positive experiences.

## Gender Differences in Consumption (Meyers-Levy & Loken, "Revisiting Gender Differences: What We Know and What Lies Ahead," Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2015; Darley & Smith, "Gender Differences in Information Processing Strategies: An Empirical Test of the Selectivity Model in Advertising Response," Journal of Advertising, 1995)

Women process advertising with greater elaboration and detail orientation (selectivity model), attending to both focal and peripheral cues, while men tend toward heuristic, schema-based processing focused on primary claims. Darley & Smith found that women are 20-30% more responsive to detailed product information and 15-20% more skeptical of broad, unsubstantiated claims. Women are also 40-50% more likely to seek social validation before purchasing (asking friends, reading reviews) than men.

When simulating: female personas should show more thorough information processing, higher review-reading rates, and greater social influence sensitivity. Male personas should show more reliance on top-line claims and brand reputation heuristics. These are population-level tendencies -- individual persona traits (personality, expertise) should modulate the strength of these effects.

## Family and Group Decision Making (Davis & Rigaux, "Perception of Marital Roles in Decision Processes," Journal of Consumer Research, 1974; Su, Fern, & Ye, "A Temporal Dynamic Model of Spousal Family Purchase-Decision Behavior," Journal of Marketing Research, 2003)

Household purchases involve complex role dynamics. Davis & Rigaux identified that 50-60% of major purchases are joint decisions, with specialization by category: groceries and children's clothing are primarily decided by one partner (historically female, though this has shifted), while financial products, automobiles, and housing are jointly decided. Su et al. found that decision influence shifts across the purchase process -- one partner may dominate problem recognition while the other dominates final choice.

When simulating: personas who are part of households should reflect joint decision-making dynamics for relevant purchase categories. A persona who personally likes a new service may still express hesitation because their partner would need to agree. Family personas should show higher price sensitivity per person (budget divided across members) but higher total household spending.
