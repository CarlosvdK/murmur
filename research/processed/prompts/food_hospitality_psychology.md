# Food & Hospitality Psychology: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer behavior for restaurants, cafes, bars, and food service businesses, apply the following empirically validated patterns. These effects are robust across cultures and price points, with specific magnitudes documented in peer-reviewed research.

## Menu Design and Pricing Psychology (Wansink, Painter, & Van Ittersum, "Descriptive Menu Labels: How Appetizing Names Increase Sales and Improve Attitudes," Cornell Hotel & Restaurant Administration Quarterly, 2001; Yang, Kimes, & Sessarego, "$ or Dollars: Effects of Menu-Price Formats on Restaurant Checks," Cornell Hospitality Report, 2009)

Descriptive menu labels ("Grandma's Zesty Lemon Chicken" vs. "Lemon Chicken") increase sales of the described item by 27% and improve taste ratings by 12%. The effect persists across repeat visits -- it is not merely novelty.

Removing dollar signs and decimal points from menu prices (e.g., "24" instead of "$24.00") increases average spend per guest by 8-12%. Numeral-only formatting reduces the psychological pain of paying by decoupling the number from currency associations.

When simulating: personas reading menus with descriptive, evocative language should show higher purchase intent and satisfaction for those items. Personas encountering numeric-only pricing should spend more freely than those seeing explicit currency symbols.

## Tipping and Gratuity Behavior (Lynn, "Tipping in Restaurants and Around the Globe: An Interdisciplinary Review," Handbook of Contemporary Behavioral Economics, 2006; Azar, "Do People Tip Strategically, to Improve Future Service? Theory and Evidence," Canadian Journal of Economics, 2007)

Tipping is driven primarily by social norm compliance (60-70% of the motivation), not service quality. The correlation between service quality and tip percentage is only r = 0.2. Tip percentages in the US average 15-20%, with high variance by demographics: younger adults tip ~16%, older adults ~18%, and income has a weaker effect than age.

When simulating: do not model tips as proportional to satisfaction. Personas tip based on social convention, with only modest adjustment for service quality. Guilt-averse personas tip higher regardless of experience. Price-sensitive personas may reduce tip percentage as the bill total rises.

## Table Turnover vs. Satisfaction (Kimes, "Restaurant Revenue Management: Implementation at Chevys Arrowhead," Cornell Hotel & Restaurant Administration Quarterly, 2004)

Restaurants face a direct tradeoff: faster table turns increase revenue per seat-hour by 15-30%, but rushed dining experiences reduce customer satisfaction by 10-20% and decrease return visit probability. Optimal dwell time varies by segment: fast-casual (30-45 min), casual dining (60-75 min), fine dining (90-120 min).

When simulating: personas who feel rushed should express lower satisfaction and reduced likelihood of returning, even if the food quality was high. Regulars are more sensitive to pace changes than first-time visitors.

## Food Presentation and Plating (Spence, Piqueras-Fiszman, Michel, & Deroy, "A Touch of Gastronomy," Flavour, 2014)

Artistic plating increases willingness-to-pay by 15-25% and taste perception ratings by 18%. The effect is strongest when visual presentation matches taste expectations (color-flavor congruence). White plates increase perceived flavor intensity relative to dark plates by approximately 10%.

When simulating: personas evaluating food service businesses should weight visual presentation as a meaningful component of perceived quality, especially for social-media-active personas who photograph meals.

## Restaurant Atmosphere and Spending (Milliman, "The Influence of Background Music on the Behavior of Restaurant Patrons," Journal of Consumer Research, 1986; North, Hargreaves, & McKendrick, "The Influence of In-Store Music on Wine Selections," Journal of Applied Psychology, 1999)

Slow-tempo background music increases average dining time by 25% and per-table spending by 29% compared to fast-tempo music. In North et al.'s wine study, French music playing in the background led to French wine outselling German wine 5:1, while German music reversed the ratio to 2:1 -- customers were unaware of the influence (only 2% attributed their choice to music).

When simulating: atmospheric factors (music, lighting, scent) should subtly shift persona spending and dwell time without the persona explicitly attributing the cause. Higher-ambiance environments increase spend even among price-conscious personas.

## Online Ordering and Delivery Psychology (Pigatto, Machado, Negreti, & Machado, "Have You Chosen Your Request? Analysis of Online Food Delivery Companies in Brazil," British Food Journal, 2017; Yeo, Goh, & Rezaei, "Consumer Experiences, Attitude and Behavioral Intention Toward Online Food Delivery Services," Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 2017)

Convenience is the primary driver of food delivery adoption (cited by 70-80% of users), followed by variety and time savings. Delivery fees above $5 reduce order probability by 30-40%. Average delivery order values are 20-35% higher than dine-in equivalents because customers cannot see social cues about portion size and add items more freely in a private digital context.

When simulating: personas ordering delivery should show higher average order values but greater sensitivity to delivery fees and estimated wait times. Satisfaction is heavily influenced by delivery time accuracy -- a meal arriving 10 minutes late reduces satisfaction more than a meal that was always quoted at the longer time.
