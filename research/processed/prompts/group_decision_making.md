# Group Decision Making: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer decisions that involve multiple people -- couples, families, friend groups, or committees -- apply the following empirically-grounded patterns. Most real-world purchase decisions are not made by individuals in isolation, and group dynamics systematically alter outcomes.

## Household Purchasing Decisions (Davis & Rigaux, "Perception of Marital Roles in Decision Processes," Journal of Consumer Research, 1974; Belch & Willis, "Family Decision at the Turn of the Century: Has the Changing Structure of Households Impacted the Family Decision-Making Process?" Journal of Consumer Behaviour, 2002)

Davis and Rigaux mapped household purchase decisions into husband-dominant, wife-dominant, and joint categories. They found that 60-70% of major household purchases involve joint decision making, with role specialization by product category: groceries and children's clothing were wife-dominant (70-80%), insurance and automotive were husband-dominant (60-70%), and vacations and housing were jointly decided (80-90%). Belch and Willis updated this framework, finding that gender-based role specialization decreased by roughly 15-20% between 1974 and 2002, with more decisions becoming joint. When simulating:
- For household-relevant purchases, simulate two decision makers rather than one. Joint decisions take 1.5-2x longer and tend to converge on safer, more moderate choices than individual decisions.
- When personas represent households, one partner may hold veto power over specific categories. A price increase that one partner tolerates may be rejected because the other partner controls that spending category.
- Joint decisions produce more conservative outcomes: roughly 20-30% less likely to adopt novel or risky options compared to individual decision makers.

## Couples and Joint Decision Making (Kirchler, "Spouses' Joint Purchase Decisions: Determinants of Influence Tactics for Muddling Through the Process," Journal of Economic Psychology, 1993; Su, Fern, & Ye, "A Temporal Dynamic Model of Spousal Family Purchase-Decision Behavior," Journal of Marketing Research, 2003)

Kirchler found that couple purchase decisions are shaped by relative influence, which depends on product importance to each partner. The partner who cares more about the outcome exerts 2-3x more influence. Su et al. documented that the decision process in couples follows a temporal pattern: initial independent preferences, followed by negotiation, followed by convergence or compromise. When simulating:
- When a purchase matters more to one partner (e.g., one spouse cares deeply about food quality, the other is indifferent), the high-interest partner should dominate the decision in approximately 70-80% of cases.
- Couples tend to avoid conflict by defaulting to the status quo. When partners disagree, roughly 40-50% of decisions result in "do nothing" or "keep what we have."
- Compromise solutions (neither partner's first choice) emerge in approximately 30-40% of joint decisions. These compromises tend to favor mid-priced, mid-risk options.

## Group Dining and Spending (Ariely & Levav, "Sequential Choice in Group Settings: Taking the Road Less Traveled and Less Enjoyed," Journal of Consumer Research, 2000)

Ariely and Levav found that when people order food sequentially in a group, they systematically diversify their choices -- choosing items they wouldn't have selected individually in order to appear unique. This variety-seeking reduced average self-reported satisfaction by approximately 10-15% compared to private ordering. The effect was strongest for individuals who ordered after hearing others' choices. When simulating:
- In group dining or group purchasing scenarios, personas who choose after others should deviate from their true preference approximately 30-40% of the time to differentiate themselves.
- This diversity seeking reduces individual satisfaction but increases group variety. Personas who ordered later in the sequence should report 10-15% lower satisfaction with their own choice.
- The effect is strongest for personas high in need for uniqueness (high Openness, high individualism) and weakest for personas high in Agreeableness who are comfortable choosing the same thing as others.
- Private or simultaneous ordering eliminates this effect. Businesses that use written orders or app-based ordering should see personas selecting their true preferences more often.

## Committee and Organizational Buying (Webster & Wind, "A General Model for Understanding Organizational Buying Behavior," Journal of Marketing, 1972; Bristor, "Influence Strategies in Organizational Buying: The Importance of Connections to the Right People in the Right Places," Journal of Business-to-Business Marketing, 1993)

Webster and Wind identified five roles in organizational buying decisions: users, influencers, buyers, deciders, and gatekeepers. Each role has different evaluation criteria and different power in the process. Bristor found that informal influence networks matter as much as formal authority: individuals with strong connections to decision makers shifted purchase outcomes by 25-35% independent of formal role. When simulating:
- For B2B or committee purchase scenarios, simulate at least 3 distinct roles with distinct priorities. Users prioritize functionality, buyers prioritize cost, and deciders balance both against strategic fit.
- Committee decisions take 3-5x longer than individual decisions and require consensus-building. The default outcome for committees facing uncertainty is to delay the decision or choose the lowest-risk vendor.
- The person who controls the agenda (gatekeeper) disproportionately influences outcomes by determining which options are even considered. If a gatekeeper excludes an option, the committee effectively never evaluates it.
- Organizational buying is more rational than consumer buying in stated criteria but equally susceptible to relationship bias, brand familiarity, and risk aversion in practice.

## Social Influence in Group Settings (Asch, "Effects of Group Pressure Upon the Modification and Distortion of Judgments," in Groups, Leadership, and Men, 1951; Cialdini & Goldstein, "Social Influence: Compliance and Conformity," Annual Review of Psychology, 2004)

Asch demonstrated that approximately 37% of responses conformed to a clearly incorrect group majority. Roughly 75% of subjects conformed at least once across 12 trials. Cialdini and Goldstein's review synthesized decades of research showing that conformity increases with group size (up to 3-4 unanimous members), perceived expertise of the group, and the individual's uncertainty about the correct answer. When simulating:
- When a persona is aware that others in their social group hold a particular opinion about a business or product, they should shift their own expressed opinion 20-30% toward the group norm.
- This conformity effect is strongest when the persona is uncertain about their own preference and weakest when the persona has strong prior experience or expertise.
- Personas high in need for social approval (high Agreeableness, high Extraversion) should conform at 1.5-2x the baseline rate. Personas high in independence (high Openness, low Agreeableness) should resist conformity and may even exhibit reactance (deliberately choosing the opposite of the group).
- Public expression of preferences produces more conformity than private expression. If the simulation involves visible choices (posting a review, choosing in front of friends), conformity pressure increases by 30-50%.

## Children's Influence on Family Purchases (McNeal, "Kids as Customers: A Handbook of Marketing to Children," 1992; Flurry & Burns, "Children's Influence in Purchase Decisions: A Social Power Theory Approach," Journal of Business Research, 2005)

McNeal estimated that children directly influence approximately $188 billion in family purchases annually (1992 figures; adjusted for inflation, substantially higher today), primarily through "pester power." Flurry and Burns found that children's influence is strongest for child-relevant products (snacks, toys, entertainment) where they exert influence in 60-80% of decisions, moderate for family products (restaurants, vacations) at 30-50%, and weakest for adult products (financial services, household maintenance) at 5-15%. When simulating:
- When a household persona has children, the children's preferences should influence restaurant and food choices in approximately 40-60% of decisions, shifting selection toward kid-friendly options.
- Businesses that cater to children (play areas, kid menus, family pricing) should see a 20-35% increase in household patronage probability, driven by reduced parent veto resistance.
- Pester power is most effective with permissive parenting styles and least effective with authoritarian styles. Roughly 30-40% of parent personas should yield to children's preferences even when it conflicts with their own, especially for low-cost items.
- For businesses considering changes that affect child-friendliness (removing a play area, eliminating a kids' menu), expect outsized negative reactions from parent personas -- 2-3x the reaction magnitude of equivalent changes to adult-focused amenities.
