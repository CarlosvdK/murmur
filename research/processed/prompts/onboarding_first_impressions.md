# Onboarding and First Impressions: Calibration Instructions

When simulating how customers react to initial encounters with a business, product, or service -- first visits, free trials, onboarding flows, and early experiences -- apply the following empirically-grounded patterns. First impressions are disproportionately sticky and shape all subsequent evaluations.

## First Impression Formation (Willis & Todorov, "First Impressions: Making Up Your Mind After a 100-ms Exposure to a Face," Psychological Science, 2006; Ambady & Rosenthal, "Half a Minute: Predicting Teacher Evaluations from Thin Slices of Nonverbal Behavior and Physical Attractiveness," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1993)

Humans form stable judgments within milliseconds. Willis and Todorov found that trait judgments made after 100ms exposure correlated at r = 0.70-0.75 with judgments made with unlimited time. Ambady and Rosenthal showed that 30-second "thin slice" observations predicted end-of-semester teaching evaluations with r = 0.76. When simulating:
- A persona's first 30-60 seconds of interaction with a business (storefront appearance, website landing page, greeting) should anchor their overall evaluation. Subsequent experiences shift this anchor by only 20-30% unless a strongly contradictory event occurs.
- Negative first impressions are harder to reverse than positive ones. Overcoming a bad first impression requires approximately 3-5 positive subsequent experiences, while a single negative experience can override a positive first impression.
- First impressions are stronger for personas with low prior knowledge. Personas who already have brand expectations (from advertising, WOM, or prior visits) are 30-40% less influenced by the first-contact experience.

## Peak-End Rule for Experience Memory (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, & Redelmeier, "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End to an Aversive Experience," Psychological Science, 1993; Fredrickson & Kahneman, "Duration Neglect in Retrospective Evaluations of Affective Episodes," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1993)

People evaluate past experiences based primarily on the peak moment (most intense point) and the ending, largely neglecting duration. Kahneman et al. demonstrated that subjects preferred objectively more painful experiences when those experiences ended on a less painful note. Fredrickson and Kahneman confirmed systematic duration neglect across multiple experimental contexts. When simulating:
- A persona's retrospective evaluation of an experience should weight the single best (or worst) moment and the final moment at roughly 2-3x the weight of the average moment.
- Duration of wait times, service encounters, or shopping experiences should be underweighted. A 45-minute visit with a great ending should be rated similarly to a 20-minute visit with a great ending, all else equal.
- Businesses that invest in strong endings (a complimentary dessert, a warm farewell, a follow-up thank-you) should see persona satisfaction scores 15-25% higher than businesses that front-load their best moments.

## Onboarding and Early Engagement (Almquist, Senior, & Bloch, "The Elements of Value," Harvard Business Review, 2016; Kumar, Pozza, & Ganesh, "Revisiting the Satisfaction-Loyalty Relationship: Empirical Generalizations and Directions for Future Research," Journal of Retailing, 2008)

Almquist et al. identified 30 "elements of value" that drive customer loyalty, with functional elements (saves time, reduces cost) as table stakes and emotional/life-changing elements as differentiators. Kumar et al. found that the strongest predictor of long-term loyalty is the quality of the first 3-6 interactions, which explained 40-50% of variance in 12-month retention. When simulating:
- The first 3 visits or interactions should carry 2-3x the weight of subsequent interactions in determining whether a persona becomes a repeat customer.
- If a persona's first experience fails to deliver on the primary value proposition (the reason they came), defection probability should be 60-75% regardless of other positive aspects.
- Personas who receive proactive communication during the early period (welcome emails, check-ins, usage tips) should show 20-30% higher engagement than those left to self-serve, particularly for complex or subscription-based services.

## Free Trial and Sampling Effects (Scott, "The Effects of Trial and Incentives on Repeat Purchase Behavior," Journal of Marketing Research, 1976; Bawa & Shoemaker, "The Effects of Free Sample Promotions on Incremental Brand Sales," Marketing Science, 2004)

Free trials and samples drive adoption, but with important caveats. Bawa and Shoemaker found that free samples increased trial rates by 15-25% but post-trial conversion to regular purchase averaged only 10-20% of samplers. Scott showed that trial driven by intrinsic interest produces 2-3x higher repeat purchase rates than trial driven by the free incentive alone. When simulating:
- When a business offers a free trial, roughly 40-60% of interested personas should try it. Of those, only 10-20% should convert to paying customers.
- Personas who tried the product because they were genuinely interested (problem-solution fit) should convert at 25-35%. Personas who tried it "because it's free" should convert at 5-10%.
- Free trial length matters: trials shorter than 7 days should show 20-30% lower conversion than 14-30 day trials, because personas haven't had time to form habits or discover full value.
- Requiring payment information upfront for a free trial should reduce trial starts by 30-50% but increase conversion of those who do trial by 2-3x (self-selection of more committed personas).

## Mere Exposure Effect on Preference (Zajonc, "Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1968; Bornstein, "Exposure and Affect: Overview and Meta-Analysis of Research, 1968-1987," Psychological Bulletin, 1989)

Repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking, even without conscious recognition. Zajonc demonstrated this across dozens of experiments with nonsense words, faces, and symbols. Bornstein's meta-analysis of 208 experiments found a mean effect size of r = 0.26, with the effect peaking at 10-20 exposures and plateauing or declining with overexposure. When simulating:
- Personas who have been exposed to a brand 5-10 times (through advertising, storefront visibility, WOM mentions) should show a 15-25% preference increase over an unfamiliar brand of equal objective quality.
- The effect peaks at 10-20 exposures. Beyond 20-25 exposures, additional exposure produces diminishing returns and may trigger annoyance in some personas (particularly those high in Reactance).
- Mere exposure works below conscious awareness. Personas should not articulate "I like it because I've seen it before." Instead, they should express vague positive affect: "it feels familiar," "I trust it more," "it just seems right."
- This effect is strongest for low-involvement decisions (choosing between similar restaurants, picking a dry cleaner) and weakest for high-involvement decisions where personas engage in deliberate comparison.
