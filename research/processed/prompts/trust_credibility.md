# Trust & Credibility: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer behavior, model trust as a central variable that mediates nearly all purchasing decisions. Trust is not binary -- it is built incrementally, maintained through consistency, and destroyed asymmetrically (much faster to lose than to gain). These dynamics are especially critical for small businesses where personal relationships carry outsized weight.

## Trust Building and Maintenance (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, "An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust," Academy of Management Review, 1995; McKnight, Choudhury, & Kacmar, "Developing and Validating Trust Measures for E-Commerce: An Integrative Typology," Information Systems Research, 2002)

Trust is composed of three factors: ability (competence to deliver), benevolence (belief the business cares about the customer), and integrity (adherence to principles the customer finds acceptable). Mayer et al. found that all three must be present for trust to form, but benevolence and integrity become more important than ability as the relationship matures. McKnight et al. demonstrated that initial trust in unfamiliar businesses relies heavily on institution-based trust (web security cues, professional appearance, third-party endorsements), which accounts for 40-55% of initial trust variance.

When simulating: new-customer personas should form initial trust based on surface cues (appearance, professionalism, reviews), with ability perceptions developing after 1-2 interactions and benevolence/integrity perceptions requiring 3-5 interactions. Established-customer personas should weight benevolence and integrity more heavily than competence when evaluating business changes.

## Brand Trust and Brand Equity (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, "The Chain of Effects from Brand Trust and Brand Affect to Brand Performance: The Role of Brand Loyalty," Journal of Marketing, 2001; Delgado-Ballester & Munuera-Aleman, "Does Brand Trust Matter to Brand Equity?," Journal of Product and Brand Management, 2005)

Brand trust directly predicts both purchase loyalty (repeat buying, beta = 0.32) and attitudinal loyalty (commitment and advocacy, beta = 0.47). Chaudhuri & Holbrook found that brand trust increases market share by 7-12% and supports a relative price premium of 8-15%. Delgado-Ballester & Munuera-Aleman demonstrated two dimensions of brand trust: reliability (consistency of performance, explaining 55% of trust variance) and intentionality (perceived customer orientation, explaining 45%).

When simulating: personas with high brand trust should tolerate price increases of 10-15% before switching, defend the brand in social conversations, and give the business the benefit of the doubt during service failures. Low-trust personas should show high price sensitivity, active comparison shopping, and rapid defection after any negative experience.

## Transparency and Authenticity Effects (Rawlins, "Give the Emperor a Mirror: Toward Developing a Stakeholder Measurement of Organizational Transparency," Journal of Public Relations Research, 2009; Gilmore & Pine, "Authenticity: What Consumers Really Want," Harvard Business Press, 2007)

Transparency -- defined as the perceived quality of intentionally shared information (completeness, accuracy, timeliness) -- increases trust by 25-35% across industries. Rawlins found three dimensions: informational (sharing relevant data), participatory (involving stakeholders in decisions), and accountability (accepting responsibility for outcomes). Gilmore & Pine documented that 85% of consumers say authenticity is important when deciding which brands to support, and perceived authenticity increases willingness-to-pay by 10-20%.

When simulating: personas should respond positively to business transparency (visible pricing, ingredient sourcing, honest about limitations) with increased trust and loyalty. However, transparency that reveals negative information is a double-edged sword: it increases trust in the messenger by 15-20% but may decrease product evaluation by 5-10%. Personas should find businesses that admit imperfections more credible than those claiming perfection.

## Crisis Communication and Trust Repair (Coombs, "Protecting Organization Reputations During a Crisis: The Development and Application of Situational Crisis Communication Theory," Corporate Reputation Review, 2007; Kim, Ferrin, Cooper, & Dirks, "Removing the Shadow of Suspicion: The Effects of Apology Versus Denial for Repairing Competence- Versus Integrity-Based Trust Violations," Journal of Applied Psychology, 2004)

Trust repair strategy must match the violation type. Kim et al. found that apologies are more effective for competence-based violations (restoring 60-70% of pre-violation trust), while denials are more effective for integrity-based violations (preserving 50-60% of pre-violation trust). This is because admitting incompetence suggests it can be fixed, while admitting dishonesty confirms a stable character flaw. Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory shows that accepting responsibility increases stakeholder trust by 20-30% when the organization is clearly at fault, but decreases trust by 10-15% when fault is ambiguous.

When simulating: after a business failure or controversy, persona trust recovery should depend on the response strategy and violation type. Personas should show faster trust recovery when the business takes responsibility for service failures (competence issues) but slower recovery when the violation involves perceived dishonesty or ethical breaches. Full trust recovery typically takes 6-12 months of consistent positive behavior, not a single apology.

## Pricing Transparency and Fairness (Xia, Monroe, & Cox, "The Price Is Unfair! A Conceptual Framework of Price Fairness Perceptions," Journal of Marketing, 2004; Bolton, Warlop, & Alba, "Consumer Perceptions of Price (Un)Fairness," Journal of Consumer Research, 2003)

Price fairness judgments are reference-dependent and heavily influenced by perceived motive. Bolton et al. found that consumers attribute 50-60% of price increases to profit-taking (even when costs have genuinely risen), and this attribution reduces purchase intent by 15-25%. Cost-justified price increases are accepted 2-3x more readily than demand-based increases. Xia et al. demonstrated that price comparison with other customers (paying more than someone else for the same thing) produces stronger unfairness reactions (30-40% stronger) than temporal comparisons (paying more than you yourself paid last time).

When simulating: personas facing price increases should evaluate fairness based on perceived reasons, not just magnitude. A 15% increase framed as "ingredient costs rose" should be accepted by 50-65% of personas, while the same increase framed as "we want to" or left unexplained should be accepted by only 25-35%. Personas who discover other customers received a better price should show disproportionately strong negative reactions, even if their own price is objectively reasonable.
