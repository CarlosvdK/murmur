# Environmental and Ethical Consumer Behavior: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer reactions to sustainability claims, ethical sourcing, local vs global brands, and corporate social responsibility, apply the following empirically-grounded patterns. The defining feature of this domain is the persistent gap between what consumers say they value and what they actually purchase.

## Green Consumer Behavior (Peattie, "Green Consumption: Behavior and Norms," Annual Review of Environment and Resources, 2010; Young, Hwang, McDonald, & Oates, "Sustainable Consumption: Green Consumer Behaviour When Purchasing Products," Sustainable Development, 2010)

Peattie's review found that while 30-50% of consumers express strong environmental concern in surveys, only 5-10% consistently translate this into purchasing behavior. Young et al. identified the key barriers: price premium, inconvenience, lack of information, and skepticism about actual environmental impact. When simulating:
- Roughly 30-40% of personas should express pro-environmental attitudes when asked. Of those, only 15-25% should consistently follow through with green purchasing behavior.
- The primary barrier for most personas is price. When the green option costs more than 10-15% above the conventional alternative, adoption drops by roughly 50%.
- Convenience is the second barrier. If the green option requires extra effort (different store, longer wait, less selection), adoption drops by an additional 30-40% beyond the price effect.
- Personas with higher education and higher income are more likely to both express and act on environmental preferences, but the attitude-behavior gap persists across all demographics.

## Attitude-Behavior Gap in Sustainability (Vermeir & Verbeke, "Sustainable Food Consumption: Exploring the Consumer Attitude-Behavioral Intention Gap," Journal of Agricultural and Environmental Ethics, 2006; Carrington, Neville, & Whitwell, "Why Ethical Consumers Don't Walk Their Talk: Towards a Framework for Understanding the Gap Between the Ethical Purchase Intentions and Actual Buying Behaviour of Ethically Minded Consumers," Journal of Business Ethics, 2010)

Vermeir and Verbeke found that while 65-70% of consumers express positive attitudes toward sustainable products, only 25-30% report regular sustainable purchasing. Carrington et al. identified implementation factors that cause the gap: competing priorities at point of purchase, lack of actual availability, price sensitivity overriding stated values, and habit overriding intention. When simulating:
- Personas should exhibit a systematic 40-60% gap between stated ethical intentions and simulated purchase behavior. If a persona says "I care about sustainability," apply a 0.4-0.6 discount to their likelihood of actually choosing the sustainable option at the point of sale.
- The gap is smallest for habituated behaviors (reusable bags, already-established brand loyalty to ethical brands) and largest for one-time or high-cost decisions.
- Situational factors close the gap: when the ethical option is equally priced, equally convenient, and highly visible at the point of decision, the gap shrinks to 10-20%.
- Social visibility also closes the gap. Personas should be 20-30% more likely to choose the ethical option when others can observe their choice.

## Willingness to Pay Premium for Ethical Products (De Pelsmacker, Driesen, & Rayp, "Do Consumers Care About Ethics? Willingness to Pay for Fair-Trade Coffee," Journal of Consumer Affairs, 2005; Trudel & Cotte, "Does It Pay to Be Good?" MIT Sloan Management Review, 2009)

De Pelsmacker et al. found that while consumers claim willingness to pay 10-30% more for fair trade products, revealed preference data shows actual premiums of only 5-10% are tolerated by most buyers. Trudel and Cotte found an asymmetric effect: consumers punish unethical products more than they reward ethical ones. Unethical behavior reduced willingness to pay by 10-15%, while ethical behavior increased it by only 5-10%. When simulating:
- Personas should tolerate ethical premiums of approximately 5-10% on routine purchases before switching to conventional alternatives. Stated willingness will be 2-3x higher than actual.
- The punishment asymmetry is critical: if a business is revealed to have unethical practices, the negative impact on purchase intent should be 1.5-2x larger than the positive impact of equivalent ethical practices.
- Premium tolerance increases with product visibility (clothing, food served to guests) and decreases with product invisibility (cleaning supplies, basic ingredients).

## Corporate Social Responsibility Effects on Purchase (Sen & Bhattacharya, "Does Doing Good Always Lead to Doing Better? Consumer Reactions to Corporate Social Responsibility," Journal of Marketing Research, 2001; Bhattacharya & Sen, "Consumer-Company Identification: A Framework for Understanding Consumers' Relationships with Companies," Journal of Marketing, 2003)

Sen and Bhattacharya found that CSR positively affects purchase intent, but only when (1) the consumer personally supports the specific cause, (2) product quality is not sacrificed, and (3) the CSR effort is perceived as genuine. When CSR is perceived as insincere, it produces a backlash that reduces purchase intent by 10-20%. Bhattacharya and Sen showed that CSR works primarily through consumer-company identification: customers buy from companies whose values they identify with. When simulating:
- CSR alignment between the persona's values and the company's CSR focus should increase purchase intent by 10-20%. Misalignment (e.g., an environmentally-focused persona encountering a company's diversity initiative) produces a neutral-to-slightly-positive effect of only 2-5%.
- Product quality must remain at or above expectations. If a persona perceives CSR as coming at the expense of quality or value, purchase intent should decrease by 5-15%.
- Greenwashing detection should trigger backlash. Approximately 25-35% of personas (higher among educated, younger, and high-Openness personas) should actively penalize perceived insincerity, reducing purchase intent by 15-25%.

## Local vs Global Brand Preference (Steenkamp, Batra, & Alden, "How Perceived Brand Globalness Creates Brand Value," Journal of International Business Studies, 2003; Batra, Ramaswamy, Alden, Steenkamp, & Ramachander, "Effects of Brand Local and Non-Local Origin on Consumer Attitudes in Developing Countries," Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2000)

Steenkamp et al. found that perceived brand globalness increases perceived quality (by approximately 15-20%) and prestige (by approximately 20-25%), but decreases perceived authenticity and local relevance. Batra et al. found that in developing economies, global brands carry a 20-30% prestige premium, while in developed economies, local brands carry a 10-15% authenticity and trust premium. When simulating:
- For small, local businesses (Murmur's primary users), personas should value localness as a positive attribute, increasing trust by 10-15% and perceived authenticity by 15-25% compared to equivalent chain or global alternatives.
- Roughly 20-30% of personas should actively prefer local businesses and express this as a meaningful factor in their decision. Another 40-50% should view it as a mild positive that is easily overridden by price or convenience.
- "Local" signaling works best for food, services, and experiential businesses. For commodity goods and technology, local origin provides little advantage and may signal lower capability.
