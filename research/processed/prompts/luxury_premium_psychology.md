# Luxury and Premium Psychology: Calibration Instructions

When simulating customer reactions to luxury, premium, or aspirational products and services, apply the following empirically-grounded patterns. These capture how real consumers perceive, desire, and justify premium purchases.

## Veblen Goods and Conspicuous Consumption (Veblen, "The Theory of the Leisure Class," 1899; Bagwell & Bernheim, "Veblen Effects in a Theory of Conspicuous Consumption," American Economic Review, 1996)

Some goods see increased demand as price rises, because the high price itself signals status to others. Bagwell and Bernheim's model demonstrates that conspicuous consumption persists even when consumers are fully rational, as long as price serves as a visible wealth signal. When simulating:
- Personas with high status-consciousness (typically higher Extraversion, higher income aspiration relative to actual income) should view price increases on visible goods as potentially enhancing desirability, not reducing it.
- This effect applies primarily to publicly consumed goods (clothing, cars, dining out) and is weak or absent for privately consumed goods (household cleaning products, underwear).
- The Veblen effect is strongest in personas who are socially mobile or status-insecure. Personas with established high status or low status-concern should respond to price increases normally (reduced demand).

## Scarcity and Luxury Perception (Verhallen & Robben, "Scarcity and Preference: An Experiment on Unavailability and Product Evaluation," Journal of Economic Psychology, 1994; Lynn, "Scarcity Effects on Value: A Quantitative Review," Journal of Economic Psychology, 1991)

Limited availability increases perceived value. Lynn's meta-analysis across 41 studies found scarcity increases desirability by approximately 20-35% on average, with stronger effects for hedonic (pleasure-oriented) than utilitarian products. Verhallen and Robben found that scarcity caused by excess demand ("popular scarcity") produces stronger preference than scarcity from limited supply. When simulating:
- Personas should rate limited-edition or limited-availability items as 20-35% more desirable than identical unlimited items.
- "Only 3 left" or "limited time" framing should increase urgency, but primarily for personas with moderate-to-high need for uniqueness.
- Personas who are skeptical or experienced shoppers should discount artificial scarcity claims by roughly 40-50%.

## Premium Pricing as Quality Signal (Rao & Monroe, "The Effect of Price, Brand Name, and Store Name on Buyers' Perceptions of Product Quality," Journal of Marketing Research, 1989; Tellis & Wernerfelt, "Competitive Price and Quality Under Asymmetric Information," Marketing Science, 1987)

Consumers use price as a heuristic for quality, especially when other quality information is unavailable. Rao and Monroe's meta-analysis found a consistent positive price-quality association with a mean effect size of r = 0.27. Tellis and Wernerfelt showed that high prices can function as credible quality signals when firms have repeat-purchase incentives. When simulating:
- When a persona lacks expertise or product information, they should infer that higher-priced options are approximately 25-30% "better" than cheaper alternatives.
- This effect weakens sharply when the persona has access to reviews, specifications, or personal experience. Expert personas should show near-zero price-quality inference.
- Cutting price on a premium product can backfire: personas may infer quality has dropped. A 30% price cut on an established premium product should trigger quality suspicion in roughly 40-50% of quality-conscious personas.

## Luxury Brand Dilution from Discounting (Keller, "Conceptualizing, Measuring, and Managing Customer-Based Brand Equity," Journal of Marketing, 1993; Aaker, "Building Strong Brands," 1996)

Frequent discounting erodes luxury brand equity. Keller's framework shows that brand associations (luxury, exclusivity, quality) are undermined when pricing signals contradict them. When simulating:
- If a luxury or premium brand offers frequent discounts (more than 2-3 times per year), loyal premium customers should express declining brand perception. Roughly 30-40% of brand-loyal personas will report feeling the brand "isn't what it used to be."
- Outlet or discount channel expansion should produce anxiety in high-loyalty premium customers, who fear their status signal is being diluted.
- Budget-conscious personas will respond positively to luxury discounts in the short term but will not develop brand loyalty from discount-driven purchases.

## Experiential Luxury vs Material Luxury (Van Boven & Gilovich, "To Do or to Have? That Is the Question," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2003; Gilovich, Kumar, & Jain, "A Wonderful Life: Experiential Consumption and the Pursuit of Happiness," Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2015)

Experiences produce more lasting satisfaction than material purchases. Van Boven and Gilovich found that 57% of respondents reported greater happiness from experiential purchases versus 34% from material purchases. Gilovich et al. identified three mechanisms: experiences become part of identity, resist unfavorable comparison, and foster social connection. When simulating:
- Personas evaluating experiential offerings (dining, spa, travel, classes) should express higher anticipated satisfaction and lower post-purchase regret than for equivalent-cost material goods.
- This effect is stronger for higher-income personas who have already satisfied material needs. For lower-income personas, material purchases that meet practical needs may still dominate.
- When a business frames a product as an experience rather than a possession (e.g., "craft your own" workshops vs. buying the finished product), expect a 15-25% increase in perceived value among experience-oriented personas.
